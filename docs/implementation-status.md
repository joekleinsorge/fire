# Implementation status

Open FI Planner is now a usable local planning application rather than a landing page or single-input calculator. It provides a multi-screen workspace backed by a shared monthly household plan. This is a substantial working implementation, while the broader five-stage product specification remains a roadmap rather than a claim of completion.

## What works

The browser workspace covers a six-input quick FI calculator with linked savings edits, a savings-rate comparison chart and expansion into a full plan, plus an overview, editable assets and debts, recurring income and spending, retirement assumptions and simulations, scenario comparison, progress observations, account settings, and child planning. Child profiles can hold separate 529 and Trump Account forecasts, including education-use assumptions and Trump Account contributions through the year the child turns 17. Child account balances never enter the parent's investable assets or FI target, but household-funded contributions (and, when enabled, education shortfalls the household covers) are paid from household cash in the account withdrawal order and reduce the parent's projection; unaffordable amounts are reported as unfunded. Employer, pilot, and contributions marked external do not affect household cash. Future purchases model one-time costs plus ongoing or time-limited upkeep in today's dollars. Open-ended upkeep raises the FI target as perpetual spending. Every cost that ends and falls after retirement starts (debt and purchase loan payments, one-time purchases net of sale proceeds, upkeep with a term or a later sale, and household child funding) is added to the target as retirement obligations, a present value discounted at the plan return. A purchase can be partly financed (a fixed-payment loan at its quoted rate whose payments start the next month and shrink in today's dollars with an expected inflation rate), kept as a non-spendable asset with an assumed growth rate, and can sell an existing account or an asset bought by an earlier purchase (less selling costs, repaying that purchase's own loan and stopping its upkeep) and pay off a debt from the proceeds, which covers moving to a new home and moving again. Each asset can be sold once, and disabled purchases never fail on stale account or debt references. The purchases table shows each purchase's effect on FI age and spendable assets at retirement, and a removal can be undone. Social Security income can be added from the monthly benefit at full retirement age shown on a Social Security statement (today's dollars) and a claiming age from 62 to 70. Full retirement age comes from the year of birth (January 1 births use the prior year), early claiming reduces the benefit by 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months before full retirement age and 5/12 of 1% for each further month, and delayed credits add 2/3 of 1% per month up to 70, following SSA's actuarial tables. With a birth date the benefit starts in the month the claiming age is attained, or the first full month at that age for a reduced benefit; it then stays level in today's dollars (cost-of-living adjustments assumed equal to inflation), enters the monthly ledger as essential income, and lowers the FI target when it is being paid at retirement start, like any other income stream. Benefits apply to full plans with accounts; the quick calculator ignores them. The Income & spending view compares the monthly benefit at 62, full retirement age, and 70 while editing. Debts are entered at their quoted rate with an optional expected inflation rate; fixed payments (calculated, or entered) then shrink in today's dollars and interest accrues at the real rate. Unpaid debt is capitalized and each shortfall is reported once, including after a loan's maturity. Child account year-end events fall in the December after a month-end valuation (a December valuation starts with the next year). A purchase scenario saves a full independent copy of the plan with its starting baseline so the two paths can be compared and either can be opened. Projections report both the FI age at or after planned retirement and the earliest age spendable assets reach the target, so trade-offs that move the crossing remain visible when retirement age is fixed. Signed-in users reopen their last plan on load, and scenarios or observations created before signing in are saved with the plan's first save. Plans can be calculated anonymously in a working session. Local accounts can save versioned plans; Settings lists saved revisions and restores older inputs as a new version. Plans also support scenario records, comparison, and progress observations, including confirmed observation removal. JSON import/export and CSV exports are available.

Planning inputs read like a plan rather than a ledger: cash flows start and end at an age (yours or a partner's) or a calendar month, re-derived when age, horizon or the valuation date change, and a healthcare helper adds age-based pre-Medicare and Medicare cost streams. A plan-wide expected inflation rate is the default for debts and purchase loans, and the projection chart and annual table can show future dollars. Edits recalculate automatically; an overview what-if panel tests retirement age, spending and return on a copy of the plan before applying them; a plan checkup lists rule-of-thumb health checks and ranks common moves by the change each makes to the earliest FI age (then a fixed shortfall, then assets at retirement less the target), projecting every move on a copy of the plan so it can be applied and undone; the overview shows year-end balances by account with net worth and Coast, Lean, regular and Fat FI milestone ages. Unsaved changes are kept in the browser and offered back on the next visit. Progress observations can be imported from CSV and are charted against the projected spendable portfolio. Simulations can use random lognormal returns, or a user-supplied series of annual real returns as rolling historical windows, resampled years, or resampled blocks; no market data is bundled. Retirement spending can follow a guardrails rule that cuts or raises discretionary spending within a floor and ceiling; simulations report how often it cut. Taxes can stay as a flat rate per account or use the 2026 federal model: pre-tax withdrawals and required minimum distributions (IRS Uniform Lifetime Table, starting at 73 or 75 by birth year) are ordinary income, the gain share of taxable-account withdrawals is a long-term capital gain stacked on ordinary income, income streams contribute a taxable share, Social Security is taxed by provisional income with its unindexed thresholds shrinking in real terms, filers 65 or older get the additional standard deduction, and an optional flat state rate applies to federal taxable income. Withdrawals withhold at the prior year's marginal rate and each December settles the year's tax from household cash; a survivor files single from the year after a death. The FI target in this model solves for the withdrawal that covers spending plus its own tax. Withdrawals follow the account list or, optionally, a tax-efficient order (cash and after-tax, then taxable, then pre-tax, then Roth). Optional Roth conversions move pre-tax savings into the first Roth account each December between chosen ages, up to the top of a chosen bracket after that year's withdrawals and taxable Social Security. A plan can include a partner with their own age, retirement age and optional life expectancy; streams and Social Security benefits belong to one person, each spouse can receive a spousal excess benefit on the other's record once both have filed (half the other's full benefit less their own, reduced 25/36 of 1% per month for the first 36 months early and 5/12 of 1% beyond, with no delayed credits), the first death ends that person's income and benefit, the survivor keeps the larger benefit, and household spending continues at a chosen survivor share.

The browser code is plain, dependency-free JavaScript split by topic (core state and helpers, timing, overview, records, assumptions, quick calculator, projection and simulation, scenarios, observations, session, and one file per feature such as purchases, children, Social Security, what-if, charts, healthcare and progress import), loaded in order with startup last.

The Python standard-library server uses SQLite for transactional persistence and a durable local job queue. It runs as a single server/worker process. Household isolation and plan roles are enforced at the API. This is a deliberate initial deployment choice instead of the target PostgreSQL/distributed-worker topology.

The monthly engine models real-dollar balances, scheduled after-tax streams, account-specific real returns and fees, availability and withdrawal order, a first-phase tax treatment per account (after-tax, pre-tax, Roth, or taxable, each with one flat withdrawal tax rate that grosses up withdrawals funding spending), debt payments, essential and desired spending outcomes, and FI target timing based on modeled retirement cash flows. Seeded simulation produces outcome denominators and annual percentile bands. Property and other assets count toward net worth but are unavailable for spending by default, with fixed assumed returns and no automatic sale or linked debt.

## Boundaries

The quick estimator assumes annual savings arrive at each projection-year end. It does not simulate retirement spending. The full-plan progress ratio uses accounts available for spending; observations map to monthly periods after the recorded valuation baseline. Scenario asset paths align on common ages only when currency and price basis match.

The calculations are planning estimates, not a ledger, tax filing, benefits determination, or guarantee. The model uses floating-point arithmetic and simplified monthly debt interest. Stream values are entered after tax. Account withdrawals pay a flat, user-entered effective rate per account (defaults: pre-tax 22%, taxable 10%, Roth and after-tax 0%), reported as `withdrawal_tax`; deposits, purchase sales, and growth are untaxed. The FI target is the after-tax target (`after_tax_target`) grossed up by `target_tax_rate`, the balance-weighted withdrawal rate of available accounts at retirement start, so it is compared with pre-tax account balances. The selected currency controls display formatting; there is no exchange-rate conversion. Users must supply return assumptions, income, expenses, benefits, healthcare costs, and other inputs they want represented.

The flat per-account rate is an assumption, not a tax calculation. The 2026 federal model omits the temporary 2025–2028 senior deduction, the net investment income tax, itemized deductions, credits, AMT, the five-year rule on converted amounts, cost-basis tracking (a fixed gain share stands in), tax on contributions or growth, state brackets, prorating a partial first projection year, and resolves the 1959 RMD-age ambiguity as 73. The app does not provide state-specific or international taxes, Social Security benefit estimates from earnings records, survivor-benefit reductions or the family maximum, the retirement earnings test, ACA/Medicare eligibility, RMD/Roth/SEPP rules, reviewed named withdrawal strategies (the guardrails rule is a simplified, user-parameterized rule), mortality tables (life expectancies are user-entered and deterministic; the survivor's own claiming-age rules are not modeled), jurisdiction-specific estate calculations, complete property transaction accounting, or professional multi-client workflows. Child account forecasts do not validate 529 contribution/program limits, qualified education expenses, state tax treatment, Trump Account eligibility or tax treatment, pilot eligibility, withdrawals, or post-18 IRA rules. Only the Trump Account contribution caps are checked: the statutory 2026–27 amounts, with later inflation-indexed caps held at the same amount in today's dollars. These are not a tax module. Trump Account contributions stop by the projected growth-period end year, while any later balance continues under the user's assumed return and fees. These areas need reviewed source modules and remain release gates.
